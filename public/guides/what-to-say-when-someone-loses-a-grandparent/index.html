What to say when someone loses a grandparent

Losing a grandparent is often someone's first real brush with grief, and the world can quietly shrink it with lines like they were old or it was expected. That framing stings, because a loved grandparent is not a small loss.

Here are things that genuinely help, things to skip, and small gestures that carry more weight than you might think.

What matters most

They may be grieving a person who was woven through their whole childhood, and they may also be scared because they have never felt this before. What helps is being told the loss is real and being reminded they are not alone in it.

What to say

“I'm so sorry. Your grandmother mattered, and this is a real loss.”
Naming it as real pushes back against everyone who is minimizing it.
“What was she like? I'd love to hear about her.”
An invitation to talk keeps the person alive in the conversation.
“There's no timeline on this. I'm here whenever you need me.”
First grief can feel confusing, and this gives them permission to feel it fully.
“I remember how you lit up talking about him.”
A specific memory shows you saw how much this person meant to them.

What to avoid

“Well, they lived a long life.”
It sounds like the loss should hurt less, and it does not.
“At least it was expected.”
Expected does not mean easy, and this can make them feel silly for grieving.
“You'll be okay, you barely saw them lately.”
It quietly ranks their grief and tells them it does not count.

Small gestures that help

Drop off something easy to eat

Food that goes straight in the fridge or freezer, no reply needed.

Send a memory in writing

A short note about their grandparent becomes something they keep.

Sit with them, no agenda

Company without pressure to talk says the loss is worth showing up for.

Check in after the funeral

A message a week later matters when everyone else has moved on.

What to say based on your relationship

A close friend
Be direct and stay close. Say the loss is real, ask about their grandparent, and keep checking in past the first week.
A younger person facing their first loss
Gently name that grief feels strange the first time and there is no wrong way to feel it. Let them know it makes sense to hurt this much.
A coworker
Keep it brief and warm. A simple I'm sorry for your loss, please take the time you need, respects their privacy without ignoring it.
Someone extremely close to their grandparent
Acknowledge the size of it. Say something like I know how much they meant to you and treat it with the weight of losing a parent, because for them it may be.

How to keep showing up

The quiet stretch comes after the cards stop, often two or three weeks in. Set a reminder to reach out then, and again near the one month mark, just to say you are thinking of them.

Common questions

What do you say when someone's grandparent dies?

Keep it warm and specific. Tell them the loss is real, invite them to share a memory, and avoid anything that shrinks their grief.

What should you not say?

Skip they lived a long life and it was expected. Those lines feel dismissive, especially when this may be someone's first big loss.

Is it okay to grieve a grandparent a lot?

Yes. A grandparent is often part of someone's entire childhood, and it can be their first real loss, so deep grief is completely normal.

What if I did not know their grandparent?

You can still help. Ask what they were like, listen, and focus on supporting your friend rather than needing a shared memory.

How do I support someone weeks later?

Reach out after the attention fades. A short message near the two week and one month marks tells them you have not forgotten.

Should I say something to a coworker?

A brief, private note of condolence is kind and appropriate. Acknowledge the loss, offer patience, and do not press for details.
